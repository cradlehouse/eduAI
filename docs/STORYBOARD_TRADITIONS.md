# Storyboarding traditions: a brief for "board style" options

## 1. Japanese e-conte (絵コンテ)

**Origin / practitioners.** Matured at Toei Doga in the 1960s–70s (Takahata, Miyazaki, Otsuka) and became the director's core document industry-wide; Toei still sells its own conte sheet as merchandise. Ghibli publishes complete e-conte volumes for every film; Hosoda and Shinkai both publish theirs (Kadokawa).

**Layout.** Portrait A4, vertical column of cuts, read top-down. Standard columns left→right: **C (cut number)** in the margin, **picture** (16:9 frame, one or more per cut), **内容 (action/content)**, **セリフ/音 (dialogue + SE)**, **時間 (duration)** written as seconds+frames (`1+12` = 36 fr), never decimals. Cut numbers run without gaps. The next document down the pipeline is the **layout sheet (レイアウト用紙)**: printed frame with peg holes, camera start/end frame boxes, arrows for move direction and speed, notes on which elements are BG vs cel. Then the **timesheet (タイムシート)**: vertical axis = time in frames (24/s, heavy rule every 6), horizontal columns = cel layers **A, B, C…** stacked bottom-up over **BG**, plus a 撮影 (camera/compositing) column for PAN, slide, fades. One sheet = 6 s (144 fr); newer 3-s sheets fit more layers.

**Notation.** `PAN`, `T.U.` / `T.B.` (track up/back), `Follow`, `FIX`, `IN/OUT`, `AC` (action cut), `画面動` (shake), `なめ` (foreground "lick" framing), `O.L.`, `F.I./F.O.`, `SL` (slide), `スーパー` (superimpose), `BANK` (reuse), `OFF`/`MONO` for off-screen/inner voice, `兼用` (shared cut). 

**Director variation.** Miyazaki writes no screenplay; the e-conte *is* the script and he draws it while production is already running. Takahata did not draw — he produced memos and 字コンテ (text conte) and had a partner draw the pictures. Hosoda draws everything by hand: grid picture + prose beside it. Shinkai draws digitally (Storyboard Pro) and is unusually explicit about "this element is 美術 (BG), this is セル" per cut. Goro Miyazaki on *Tales from Earthsea* deliberately borrowed Pixar's pinnable boards for review, then transferred to standard conte paper once timing was locked — a useful precedent that the two traditions can be staged.

**Optimises for:** timing and cut economy (every cut has a number and a duration; total runtime is summed from the column), layer separation, camera instruction.

**Steal:** the seconds+frames duration field and running total; the A/B/C-over-BG layer notation in the timesheet — this is exactly your background-plate / character-pass structure.

## 2. American studio animation (Disney → Pixar; Warner; DreamWorks)

**Origin.** Webb Smith at Disney pinned sequential sketches on his wall (~1931–33); Walt ordered corkboards and created a separate story department — a new job distinct from animator. *Three Little Pigs* (1933) is the first fully boarded short. Joe Grant coloured his pastel story sketches to win pitches; Bill Peet's shouting matches with Walt in story meetings are legend.

**Layout.** Landscape 4:3/16:9 panels on index-card-sized sheets pinned in rows on 4×8-ft boards; the *page* is a wall, not a sheet. Printed studio templates (Cartoon Network / Nickelodeon-style) put 3 panels per landscape page with **Scene / Panel / Action / Dialogue** fields beneath, plus BG and camera notes. Panels are cheap and plentiful (Pixar: 4,000+ per feature; 6–8 full story-reel passes before animation).

**Pipeline.** Beat outline → **beat boards** (one image per story point, no camera choreography) → sequence boards → **pitch** (the artist performs the sequence live, acting voices and SFX, to the director) → editorial cuts boards with scratch dialogue into a **story reel / Leica reel / animatic** → iterate. Warner's Chuck Jones unit split story/timing (Jones, via exposure sheets) from layout (Maurice Noble, who traced all character poses onto one sheet then designed the BG around them).

**Optimises for:** performance, emotional clarity, throwaway iteration, group critique.

**Steal:** the pitch as a first-class artefact (a "pitch mode" that plays the board with the student's recorded narration), and beat boards as a pre-storyboard layer.

## 3. Live-action film

**Practitioners.** Hitchcock planned so completely on paper that he told Ebert "when you finish the script, the film is perfect" and considered shooting a 40% loss; Saul Bass's 48 shower-scene panels for *Psycho* were shot nearly as drawn. Ridley Scott's "Ridleygrams" are dense contour-shaded thumbnails in a Moebius-influenced hand, drawn by the director himself. Spielberg and Kubrick used boards selectively (inference: for action/VFX sequences rather than whole films).

**Documents.** (a) **Storyboard** — typically 3 landscape frames per portrait page, shot number, lens/move arrows, description beneath. (b) **Shot list** — table: scene, shot, size (WS/MS/CU), angle, movement, lens, notes. (c) **Overhead / floor plan** — bird's-eye diagram of set, actors' blocking, camera positions and numbered setups; "a storyboard is what the frame looks like, an overhead is where things are." (d) **AD's script breakdown** — page divided into **eighths**, every scene tagged with cast, props, wardrobe, SFX; strips sorted onto a **stripboard** (colour = INT/EXT, day/night) to build the shooting schedule. Rough rule: ~20 min per eighth, but "not all eighths are equal."

**Optimises for:** coverage, logistics, cost.

**Steal:** the overhead diagram per scene and the shot-list table view; the eighths → schedule idea maps onto your cut list as a production-effort estimate.

## 4. European and other animation

- **Soyuzmultfilm / Norstein.** Cut-out figures on stacked glass planes under a multiplane camera; tracing-paper fog layers. Production was largely improvised under camera with designer Francheska Yarbusova; Kitson's monograph reconstructs scenes "in the order they emerged in the director's mind" rather than from a fixed board. Lesson: a *layer-stack* plan (what's on each glass plane) mattered more than the panel sequence.
- **Czech (Trnka).** Puppet studio built armature skeletons; drama conveyed via body language, lighting and camera moves. Planning documents not well published in English (flag: inference that boards were secondary to puppet/set blocking).
- **Halas & Batchelor (*Animal Farm*, 1954).** Batchelor scripted; both did boards/characters/layout; a **tension chart** mapped the emotional arc with music and colour indications alongside the storyboard; ex-Disney John Reed's layouts and "sweatbox notes" governed accuracy.
- **Aardman.** *The Wrong Trousers* published board: **six frames per page**, scene + shot numbers, animator notes, frame timing, dialogue, footnotes; boards → animatic → posing/lighting/timing. Stop-motion makes the board almost a shot list because re-shoots are ruinous.
- **Chomet.** Bande-dessinée sensibility: staging designed as comic-page composition first, camera second (AWN: he "marries comics and film").
- **Bollywood song picturisation.** Music is recorded first; the sequence is shot to playback with a choreographer (dance director) breaking the song into shots — the inverse of your "music scored to the locked cut." Sources are thin on paper formats; relevance is conceptual (music-first board).

**Steal:** Halas & Batchelor's tension chart as a strip above the board; Aardman's six-up page as a compact "shot-locked" view.

## 5. Comics and manga as pedagogy

Eisner's *Comics and Sequential Art* and McCloud's *Understanding Comics* frame the panel grid as a thinking tool: the **gutter** is where the reader supplies time; McCloud's six transition types (moment-to-moment … non-sequitur) are effectively a taxonomy of cuts. Manga *nēmu* (thumbnail page) is the manga analogue of the beat board. **Webtoon** vertical scroll (800 px wide, single column) replaces the page turn with **gutter height as timing**: ~200 px between panels normally, 600–1,000 px for scene changes, short gaps for action, long gaps before an emotional beat — a scroll-position-as-time model that is structurally closest to your vertical e-conte.

**Steal:** label each cut-to-cut transition with a McCloud type; let vertical whitespace between cuts encode duration in a "scroll" view.

## 6. Games, previs, and TV animation

**Previs (The Third Floor, Unreal).** 3D "master scenes" of 5–10 min, lensed interactively; output = shot list plus **techvis** (camera height/speed, green-screen dimensions, eyeline heights — "from a single PDF to hundreds of schematic diagrams") so decisions survive to set; **postvis** after shooting. Games (Naughty Dog) previs cinematics through iteration then mocap. "Shot bible" is a common in-house term but no public spec surfaced (flag).

**Board-driven vs script-driven TV.** Script-driven (*Simpsons*, *Fairly OddParents*): full script → recorded cast → board artists illustrate. Board-driven (*Adventure Time*, Frederator shows): short outline → board artists write dialogue and gags in the panels → board is turned into a script for recording. Board artists are writers; the page has the same Scene/Panel/Action/Dialogue fields but dialogue is authored there.

**Steal:** a per-cut "lens/height/move" techvis block; a cohort toggle for board-driven (dialogue editable on the board) vs script-driven (dialogue locked from the script).

## 7. Film-school pedagogy

- **Sheridan** (course outlines public): ANIM20146 grades Beat Boards 10%, Reverse Pitch 10%, Storyboard-from-provided-story 20%, Beat Boards 10%, Original Pitch 10%, Original Storyboard 20%, Leica Reel 20%. ANIM30102: Premise 10%, Group Story Development 30%, Storyboard Test 20%, Conformed Board/Story Reel 20%, Story Analysis 20%. Outcomes stress visual consistency across a group and a story reel with "effective sound, timing, recording, editing and performance."
- **Gobelins** short course: storyboard → edited animatic with sound; instruction leans on gesture drawing and editing.
- **CalArts** Beginning Story Development: thumbnails, character posing, layout, portfolio (syllabus behind paywall; details inferred from description).
- **USC CTPR 507 / NYU Sight & Sound**: crews of four make short silent-then-sound films; storyboards and floor plans introduced as exercises grow (inference from course descriptions; no rubric surfaced).

Common hand-ins: beat boards → boards → pitch → animatic/Leica reel; a timed reel with sound is the summative artefact everywhere.

## Proposed board styles

| Style | Tradition | Layout | Who picks it | Data fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Conte** (current) | Ghibli e-conte | Vertical cuts; C#, picture, action, dialogue/SE, sec+frames | Anime-leaning cohorts; timing discipline | Clean: cuts, notes, layers, timing |
| **Pitch Wall** | Disney/Pixar | Landscape panels in rows, 3-up pages, Scene/Panel/Action/Dialogue; beat-board tier; play-through pitch mode | Story-first instructors, group critique | Needs: beat-board tier, panel-per-cut (many panels per cut), recorded pitch |
| **Shot Deck** | Hollywood live-action | Shot-list table + overhead diagram per scene + 3-panel pages; eighths estimate | Live-action / hybrid cohorts, AD/producer roles | Needs: shot size/angle/lens fields, overhead canvas, scene breakdown tags |
| **Six-Up** | Aardman | Six frames per page, shot#, frame count, animator notes | Stop-motion or locked-shot projects | Clean; add footnotes field |
| **Scroll** | Webtoon + McCloud | Single column, gutter height = duration, transition-type label per cut | Comics-native teens; pacing lessons | Clean; add transition type; derived from timing |
| **Board-Driven** | Adventure Time | Pitch Wall variant where dialogue is authored in-panel and exported to a script | Writing-through-drawing cohorts | Needs: dialogue-as-source-of-truth flag, script export |

Optional overlay for any style: Halas & Batchelor **tension chart** (emotion/music/colour strip) — needs a per-cut intensity field.
